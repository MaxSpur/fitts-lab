Method

Distance, width, and time

Fitts’ law relates target distance and width to pointing time. These short tasks illustrate the relationship; they are not a device benchmark.

01 / Distance and precision

For the same width, a more distant target is generally harder to acquire. A wider target tolerates more endpoint variation. The ratio D/W combines both into an index of difficulty.

02 / A target has a shape

A circle offers the same diameter in every approach direction. For rectangles, this demo can calculate the center-directed chord along your approach. It is one geometric approximation, not a universal 2D model.

03 / An edge changes the task

A menu bar with a hard upper boundary absorbs outward vertical overshoot. A corner adds a second constraint. You still have to travel there, and a menu item still requires horizontal precision.

The edge limit

In one dimension, an unbounded target can absorb overshoot beyond its near edge. Extend the far edge while keeping the near edge fixed.

IDoriginal = log₂ [(dfar + dnear) / (dfar − dnear)]

The original Fitts difficulty term approaches zero in that limit. Movement time and effort do not: the pointer still travels to the near edge. A real menu also requires accuracy along the edge.

Why the other formula differs

For a finite interval, D = (dnear + dfar)/2 and W = dfar − dnear. The original expression is log₂(2D/W); Welford uses log₂(D/W + ½). Both tend to zero in this limit. The Shannon expression used in the ordinary-target plots instead tends to log₂(1.5). These are different formulae applied to an idealized interval.

This limit is not a prediction for movement time or a complete two-dimensional corner model. Edge conditions are compared separately and excluded from the ordinary Fitts regression.

What is measured, what is shared, and what can this tell us?

The acquisition time runs from activation of a target to a primary-button press. It includes reaction and any correction; it is not a movement-onset measurement. Starting clicks are unscored. A miss stays in the data; the target remains active until a successful click. Retry attempts are identifiable separately. Pausing invalidates only the unfinished movement, with an interruption entry retained in the set log.

Distances and coordinates are CSS pixels in the experiment area. Interface boundaries are simulated through Pointer Lock; OS cursor acceleration remains in use. Device categories are self-reported. Different devices and display configurations can have different intercepts and slopes. These short sets are a classroom illustration, not a device benchmark or a controlled experiment.

Completed attempts, sampled trajectories, settings, and set history are saved in this browser’s IndexedDB. Storage can be cleared or evicted by the browser, so export important sessions. Joining a classroom shares only new attempts made afterward, with a random participant label and a coarse device category. It shares sampled paths, geometry, timings, and device-pixel ratio—not your name, email, keystrokes, or screen contents. Hosting services can retain network access logs. Stop sharing before returning to individual exploration.

Throughput and statistically effective width are deliberately omitted from this short demo: sparse sets and clamped endpoints make them easy to misinterpret. Source references and further mathematical notes are in Methods.
